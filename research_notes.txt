1. THE CORE QUESTION THE PAPER IS ORGANIZED AROUND
================================================================
Given a data stream landing in one axis-aligned box (a "cell" of the
adaptive grid), approximately answer: is the local data equidistributed
(uniform) in this cell, or not? This is framed as the first of three
separable sub-algorithms, each independently analyzable:

  1. EQUIDISTRIBUTION TEST: is this cell's data locally uniform?
  2. EXPANSION RULE: once judged non-uniform, how to split (which axis/axes,
     not necessarily all of them at once)?
  3. CELL AWARENESS: do neighboring cells agree with each other, so a
     contraction/merge is justified even if neither looks uniform in
     isolation?

Framing all three as instances of one underlying template turned out to be
the useful unifying idea (see section 4).

2. THE ARCHITECTURAL WALL FOUND IN THE EXISTING CODE
================================================================
`AdaptiveDStream._split()` (src/adaptive_dstream/model.py) splits every
dimension of a cell at once: `itertools.product([0,1], repeat=dim)`, i.e.
one split produces 2**dim children. This is confirmed expensive even in the
repo's own comments (run_dimension_sweep.py: dim=10 at n=600 took "over two
minutes" during calibration).

Consequence: `_should_split` gates every split behind
`projected_leaf_count <= max_cells` BEFORE the split happens. With the
default max_cells=2500, 2**dim already exceeds 2500 once dim >= 12
(2**12 = 4096), so from dim>=12 on, the root is architecturally unable to
split even once -- it degenerates to "the whole domain is one cell",
regardless of what any uniformity criterion says. This was verified
empirically this session (see section 6).

KDD Cup 99 has ~41 features -- confirmed correct. This means: before any
better equidistribution *test* matters, the *split rule* itself needs to
stop being full-orthant. Candidate fixes discussed (not yet implemented):
  - binary/kd-tree-style split on a single chosen axis
  - split only the top-k axes ranked by per-axis non-uniformity evidence
  - split along the top principal direction of a tracked covariance matrix
  - random/hashed subspace splitting
This is flagged as a PREREQUISITE for reaching real-data dimensionality
(d~40), separate from and prior to the choice of test statistic.

3. CANDIDATE EQUIDISTRIBUTION TESTS DISCUSSED (BEYOND MEAN/VARIANCE)
================================================================
Current production criterion (cell.py: GridCell.refinement_score): compares
tracked mean to cell center and tracked variance to the uniform value
1/12 * side_length^2. Cheap (O(1) streaming update via decayed sufficient
stats s0/s1/s2), but a pure moment proxy -- blind to same-mean/same-variance
shape differences (e.g. correlation, symmetric bimodality), and has no
formal null distribution (its split_threshold is a tuned constant).

Alternatives discussed, roughly in the order raised:

- HIGHER MOMENTS (skewness, kurtosis): cheapest extension, same streaming
  machinery (add s3/s4). Uniform's excess kurtosis is exactly -6/5, so a
  bimodal-but-same-variance shape is visible here even though variance
  alone misses it.

- CHI-SQUARE / ENTROPY ON SUB-ORTHANTS: bin into the same 2**d children the
  split would produce, compare counts to n/2**d. Has a real asymptotic
  null (chi-square), but shares the split rule's exponential-in-d cost --
  same wall as section 2 for the *test*, not just the split.

- SPACE-FILLING CURVE + 1D TEST: Hilbert-curve map to a scalar, then a
  classic 1D spacings/KS test. Sidesteps the 2**d blowup; needs an
  order-statistics streaming sketch (KLL, t-digest, or a bottom-k/KMV
  sketch -- see section 4).

- RANDOM PROJECTIONS: project onto a few random directions; the null for a
  projected uniform box is a generalized Irwin-Hall distribution (closed
  form; Gaussian by CLT as d grows, mean = v.center, variance =
  sum(v_i^2 * w_i^2)/12). Catches correlation/diagonal structure that
  per-axis tests miss by construction. NOTE: a random projection binned
  into width-w buckets IS the standard Euclidean LSH family
  (Datar-Immorlica-Indyk-Mirrokni 2004, "p-stable LSH") -- these are the
  same tool, not two separate ones.

- DISCREPANCY THEORY (star / L2-discrepancy): the actual formal definition
  of "equidistributed" (Weyl's equidistribution theorem: D_n* -> 0). L2
  discrepancy has a closed form via pairwise point distances (Warnock's
  formula, 1972), O(n^2*d) -- too expensive to run live, but valuable as an
  OFFLINE ground-truth benchmark: validate cheap streaming proxies against
  it on synthetic data with dial-able non-uniformity (Spearman correlation
  or AUC).

- SPATIAL STATISTICS / COMPLETE SPATIAL RANDOMNESS (CSR): reframe "is this
  uniform" as "is this consistent with a homogeneous Poisson process".
  Ripley's K-function (K(r) proportional to r^d under CSR) / L-function,
  or the cheaper Clark-Evans nearest-neighbor index (has a known
  asymptotically-normal null -> real z-score). Important distinction this
  framing gives that variance alone cannot: CLUSTERING (points closer than
  CSR predicts, the thing we want to detect) vs REGULARITY/INHIBITION
  (points more evenly spread than random, e.g. near-lattice) -- both are
  "not uniform" by variance but are opposite phenomena, and only one
  probably warrants splitting.

- PROPERTY TESTING / DISTRIBUTION TESTING (theoretical CS): the field whose
  literal subject is "approximately decide if a distribution is uniform"
  with sublinear sample complexity and formal soundness/completeness
  guarantees (Batu-Fischer-Rubinfeld, Paninski). Canonne's survey "Topics
  and Techniques in Distribution Testing" is the recommended entry point --
  flagged as genuinely worth reading, not just citing.

- WEYL SUMS / EQUIDISTRIBUTION THEOREM (IMPLEMENTED THIS SESSION): see
  section 5. Weyl's 1916 criterion: a sequence is equidistributed iff
  (1/n) sum exp(2*pi*i*k.x_j) -> 0 for every nonzero integer frequency
  vector k. This is the headline new criterion added this session, with a
  genuine asymptotic chi-square null (see section 5).

- HIGHER-ORDER FOURIER ANALYSIS / GOWERS NORMS U^k: the theoretically
  "correct" next rung above linear Weyl sums / random projections, needed
  to catch structure invisible to any linear functional (e.g. data
  concentrated on a curved/quadratic manifold). DELIBERATELY NOT PURSUED:
  no efficient closed-form/algorithm for U^k, k>=3, on continuous point
  clouds (native setting is finite abelian groups / ergodic systems, not
  R^d boxes); would need discretization first, reintroducing the curse of
  dimensionality it's meant to avoid. Recommended treatment: mention in
  related-work/future-work as the top of the hierarchy this line of work
  sits at the bottom of (0th order = mean/variance, 1st order = Weyl
  sums/random projections, higher order = Gowers norms), not implement.
  Cheaper proxy for the same failure mode (curved-manifold concentration):
  local PCA eigenvalue gaps, or projecting onto pairwise-product features
  x_i*x_j before a 1D test.

4. THE HASH-BASED UNIFYING TEMPLATE
================================================================
Traced to Sanjeev Arora's COS521 (Princeton) Lecture 5, "Hashing with real
numbers" (https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall14/cos521/lecnotes/lec5.pdf).
That lecture is specifically about two things (not a general JL/LSH survey):
  (a) cardinality estimation via min-hash registers (Flajolet-Martin,
      1985): hash to [0,1], keep the running minimum, use
      E[min of N uniforms] = 1/(N+1) to back out N.
  (b) MinHash / Jaccard similarity estimation (Broder, 1997): hash of a set
      = min hash value over its elements; collision probability between two
      sets' minhashes exactly equals their Jaccard similarity.

The transferable insight: (a) is a template --
  "push each point through a hash/transform phi; maintain an O(1) streaming
   register of phi-values (a min, a mean, a bucket count); compare the
   register's observed value to its closed-form value under the uniform
   null; the gap is the non-uniformity statistic."
Every criterion discussed (moments, chi-square-on-buckets, random
projections/LSH, Weyl sums, order-statistics/bottom-k sketches) is one
instantiation of this same template, differing only in phi and which
moment of the register is checked -- worth stating as one unifying lemma in
the paper rather than presenting them as unrelated alternatives.

(b)'s MinHash/Jaccard idea repurposes directly for CROSS-CELL AWARENESS
(sub-problem 3, section 1): represent each cell's occupied fine sub-buckets
as a set, take a MinHash signature, estimate Jaccard similarity between two
face-adjacent cells' signatures without comparing full point sets. High
similarity => the split boundary isn't doing anything => merge candidate.
NOT YET IMPLEMENTED -- flagged as the natural next addition for
sub-problem 3, which the current code (model.py: _contract_recursive) does
not address at all (it only looks at a cell's own children's scores, never
at neighbors, despite already computing face-adjacency for clustering via
_cells_touch_by_face).

Also relevant, not yet implemented: bottom-k / K-Minimum-Values (KMV)
sketches (Bar-Yossef et al. 2002; Cohen 1997) generalize the single-min
Flajolet-Martin register to a small set of order statistics, giving a
richer, rigorously-bounded streaming uniformity test (spacings/Greenwood
statistic) than tracking only the minimum.

Count-Min sketch: infrastructure, not a test by itself. One-sided
overestimation via collisions makes it good at flagging heavy hitters/point
masses (exactly the corner_spike scenario, section 6), but collisions blur
the fine structure needed to tell "uniform" from "slightly lumpy" -- treat
as a secondary spike-detector layered on top of a real uniformity test, not
the primary criterion.

5. WEYL EQUIDISTRIBUTION TEST -- IMPLEMENTED AND VALIDATED THIS SESSION
================================================================
File: src/adaptive_dstream/criteria.py, function
weyl_equidistribution_score() (+ default_weyl_frequencies, weyl_threshold).

Math (full derivation is in the module docstring): rescale a cell's points
into u in [0,1]^dim. For a nonzero INTEGER frequency vector k, the
characters exp(2*pi*i*k.u) are an orthonormal basis of L^2([0,1]^d), which
gives, under u ~ Uniform([0,1]^d), EXACTLY (not just asymptotically):
  E[phi_k] = 0,  E[|phi_k|^2] = 1  =>  Var(Re phi_k)=Var(Im phi_k)=1/2
and for two distinct nonzero integer k != +-k', every cross second moment
between (Re phi_k, Im phi_k) and (Re phi_k', Im phi_k') is exactly zero too.
So the per-frequency statistic T_k = 2*n*|S_k|^2 (S_k = sample mean of
phi_k) is asymptotically chi-square(2), and summed across m frequencies,
T = sum_k T_k is asymptotically chi-square(2m) under H0 -- a REAL
significance level, not a tuned constant. This directly answers "how do
you choose the threshold" for this criterion (section 8).

Default frequency set (default_weyl_frequencies): d axis-unit vectors
(catches per-axis marginal non-uniformity) + for each consecutive axis pair,
a "sum" and "difference" frequency (catches correlation/diagonal
structure) -- size grows LINEARLY in d (3d-2), unlike a 2**d orthant test.
Cost is O(n*d) per cell, not O(n*2**d) -- this specifically fixes the
dimensionality problem for the TEST (independent of, and does not by
itself fix, the split-rule wall in section 2).

VALIDATED empirically (this session): empirical false-positive rate under
true uniformity tracks the nominal alpha=0.05 closely across dim in
{2,5,10,20} and n in {100,500,2000} (observed range ~0.025-0.07,
consistently close to 0.05) -- the calibration is real, not just asymptotic
theory. See outputs/cell_equidistribution_calibration.png.

6. SYNTHETIC SINGLE-CELL SHAPES ADDED (PHASE 1 EVALUATION)
================================================================
File: src/adaptive_dstream/cell_shapes.py -- generators for exactly the
"is this ONE cell equidistributed" question, separate from the existing
multi-phase streaming generators in synthetic.py (which are for the
end-to-end clustering evaluation, "phase 2"). All return points in the unit
box [0,1]^dim.

  - uniform: H0 baseline.
  - core_border_imbalance: uniform core + two thin border slabs at OPPOSITE
    faces with DIFFERENT densities from each other (the paper's headline
    stress case -- "a cluster's center looks fine, its two edges disagree
    with each other"). axis="diag" variant places it along the main
    diagonal via a shared additive shift -- empirically this did NOT turn
    out to be axis-blind (both criteria detect it at AUC~1; the shared
    shift still perturbs every marginal), kept as an easier sanity check,
    not the hard case.
  - diagonal_correlated: points in a thin band around the main diagonal --
    THIS is the shape that actually isolates the axis-blind case cleanly
    (see results below).
  - symmetric_bimodal: two Gaussian blobs symmetric about the box center
    along one axis -- "same moments (roughly), different shape" case.
  - corner_spike: point-mass concentration in one corner + uniform
    background -- the heavy-hitter/Count-Min-relevant case.

Evaluation script: examples/run_cell_equidistribution_sweep.py. Compares
moment_refinement_score vs weyl_equidistribution_score via ROC-AUC
(uniform vs. each shape) across dim in {2,5,10,15,20}, n=400, 60 repeats
per cell; plus a false-positive-rate calibration sweep at dim=10 across
n in {50,...,6400}. Outputs: outputs/cell_equidistribution_auc.{json,png},
outputs/cell_equidistribution_calibration.png.

HEADLINE RESULTS (this session's run, R=60 repeats, n=400):
  - core_border_imbalance:      moment AUC 0.97-1.00, weyl AUC 0.99-1.00  (both catch it, "easy" case)
  - core_border_imbalance_diag: moment AUC 1.00,       weyl AUC 1.00       (both catch it -- not actually the hard case, see above)
  - diagonal_correlated:        moment AUC 0.38-0.54  (CHANCE LEVEL, every dim tested)
                                 weyl   AUC 1.00        (PERFECT, every dim tested, d=2 through d=20)
    => clean, dimension-independent demonstration that mean/variance is
       structurally blind to correlation-driven non-uniformity, exactly as
       predicted theoretically, while the Weyl test (via its
       sum/difference cross-axis frequencies) catches it completely.
  - symmetric_bimodal:          moment AUC 0.78-0.995 (good, catches it)
                                 weyl   AUC 0.43-0.53  (near chance -- see caveat below)
  - corner_spike:                moment AUC 1.00, weyl AUC 1.00 (both catch it, "easy" case)

IMPORTANT CAVEAT on symmetric_bimodal (verified, not a bug): the default
separation=0.25 for that shape lands EXACTLY on a null harmonic of the k=1
Weyl frequency -- cos(2*pi*0.25) = 0 exactly, so the two symmetric blobs at
0.5+-0.25 produce IDENTICAL cos(2*pi*x) values and the k=1 frequency sees
zero signal by pure coincidence of the chosen parameter. This is a real,
general, citable property of Fourier/Neyman-smooth goodness-of-fit tests:
a finite frequency set has "blind directions" -- alternatives whose signal
lies entirely in an untested harmonic (here, k=2 would have caught it,
cos(4*pi*0.25) = cos(pi) = -1, but k=2 is not in the default frequency
set). CONCLUSION FOR THE PAPER: moment-based and Weyl-based criteria have
DIFFERENT, complementary blind spots -- neither dominates uniformly. This
is a stronger, more defensible finding than "the new criterion wins".
CONFIRMED: re-ran symmetric_bimodal at separation=0.15 (cos(2*pi*0.15)=0.588,
well off the k=1 null) at dim in {5,10}, R=60: weyl AUC jumped from
0.45-0.49 (at separation=0.25) to 1.000, matching moment's AUC of 1.000 at
the same setting. This confirms the blind spot is exactly the mechanism
described above (a coincidental alignment with the default parameter, not a
general weakness of the Weyl test) and is worth stating explicitly as a
controlled ablation in the paper, not just the raw separation=0.25 numbers.
Not yet done: extend default_weyl_frequencies with k=2 harmonics and
re-measure at separation=0.25 (raises dof/m, a real power/multiple-testing
tradeoff worth characterizing rather than just adding).

8. THRESHOLD SELECTION -- THE GENERAL ANSWER
================================================================
User's question: "algorithms are sensitive to threshold choice -- how do
you choose the right one?"

General recipe (Neyman-Pearson-style calibration), demonstrated concretely
this session for the Weyl criterion: derive or simulate the statistic's
null distribution (here: chi-square(2m), derived exactly, see section 5),
pick the threshold as its (1-alpha) quantile for a chosen significance
level alpha, and VALIDATE via simulation that the achieved false-positive
rate actually matches the nominal alpha (done -- section 5, calibration
plot). This generalizes to any ad hoc statistic that lacks a closed-form
null: simulate the statistic under a realistic H0 (uniform-in-a-box, at the
sample sizes/decay actually used), and set the threshold to the empirical
(1-alpha) quantile of that simulated null, then re-validate periodically
since decay/sample-size assumptions can drift.

Empirical finding this session for the CURRENT moment-score threshold
(0.04, decay=0.995): under true uniformity, the batch (no-decay) version of
the score shrinks like the estimator's own O(1/sqrt(n)) convergence, so
false-positive rate at threshold 0.04 was measured at essentially 0 even at
n=12800 -- no "more data causes spurious splits" paradox in the batch
setting.

CONFIRMED via the decayed-streaming version (GridCell.update with decay,
T=20000 points, dim=10, 100 repeats per decay value) -- the "paradox" is
real, but it is governed by DECAY (which caps effective sample size at
~1/(1-decay), so the noise floor never shrinks no matter how long the
stream runs), not by raw data volume:

    decay   eff_n~1/(1-decay)   P(spurious split over T=20000)   max score seen
    0.995   200                 0.000                            0.027
    0.99    100                 0.000                            0.027
    0.95     20                 0.000                            0.029
    0.9      10                 0.020                            0.041
    0.8       5                 1.000                            0.068

At the repo's production defaults (decay=0.995, threshold=0.04) the method
is safe -- but only because 0.04 happens to sit far above the noise floor
at that specific decay. That safety margin is an ACCIDENT of the chosen
constant, not a property of the method: push decay to 0.9 (a reasonable
choice if faster drift-adaptation is wanted) and spurious splits start
appearing; push it to 0.8 and every run eventually splits a perfectly
uniform cell. This is exactly the general point in this section: a
principled threshold would be derived from the known effective sample size
1/(1-decay) (computable in closed form for geometric decay) and the number
of times the criterion gets evaluated, the way the Weyl test's chi-square
threshold is derived from n and m -- not a single constant assumed to work
across all decay settings.

12. RANDOM PROJECTION TEST -- ADDED IN THE FOLLOW-UP SESSION
================================================================
User pushback (correct): Weyl (integer frequencies, periodic phi_k =
exp(2*pi*i*k.u)) is NOT the same thing as a classical "random projection"
test (real-valued direction v, raw linear value y = v.u, test its full
distribution). The earlier writeup conflated them. Implemented random
projections properly this session:
src/adaptive_dstream/criteria.py: random_projection_score() +
default_random_directions().

Math: for a fixed unit vector v, Y = v.u has, under H0, E[Y] = 0.5*sum(v)
and Var(Y) = ||v||^2/12 = 1/12 EXACTLY, independent of v and dim (since
||v||=1). Y is bounded in a known range R(v) = sum(|v_j|). Applied
Hoeffding's inequality (1963) directly to the sample mean of Y (mean
sub-test) and to Z=(Y-0.5*sum(v))^2 in [0,R(v)^2] (variance sub-test, using
the *known* null mean so Z stays i.i.d.-bounded). m directions x 2
sub-tests = 2m elementary tests, each run at level alpha/(2m); a union
bound gives P(any elementary test rejects | H0) <= alpha at EVERY finite n
-- an exact, non-asymptotic, distribution-free guarantee, unlike Weyl's
asymptotic chi-square. m defaults to the same count as
default_weyl_frequencies(dim) for a fair equal-budget comparison.

VALIDATED: empirical FPR was measured at 0.000 (R=400 reps) across dim in
{2,5,10,20}, n in {100,500,2000} -- i.e. strictly under the nominal bound
of 0.05, confirming Hoeffding's bound is valid AND, as expected, loose
(conservative).

THREE-WAY AUC COMPARISON (n=400, R=60, dims 2-20; see
outputs/cell_equidistribution_auc.png, regenerated):

  - corner_spike:                 moment=weyl=randproj=1.00 (all three trivially catch a blatant point mass)
  - core_border_imbalance:        moment~0.99, weyl~1.00, randproj 1.00 at d=2 COLLAPSING to 0.50 by d=10+
  - core_border_imbalance_diag:   moment=weyl=1.00 everywhere, randproj 1.00 at d=2 decaying to ~0.68 at d=20
  - diagonal_correlated:          weyl=1.00 everywhere; moment AND randproj BOTH at chance (~0.4-0.54) everywhere
  - symmetric_bimodal:            moment 0.82-1.00 (best here); weyl and randproj both near chance (weyl's
                                   miss is the known separation=0.25 null-harmonic coincidence, section 6;
                                   randproj's is structural -- see below)

KEY FINDING: random projection (Hoeffding-bounded, linear, mean+variance
sub-tests) is essentially POWERLESS (AUC ~0.5) on every shape except the
crudest one (corner_spike), and its power on the border-imbalance shapes
COLLAPSES as dimension grows (1.0 at d=2 -> 0.5 by d=10-15). This is not a
bug, it is the direct, measurable cost of the finite-sample guarantee: for
a random unit vector in dim d, R(v)=sum|v_j| grows roughly like sqrt(dim)
(each |v_j| ~ 1/sqrt(dim) on average), and Hoeffding's tail bound has R^2
in the denominator of its exponent -- so the same true deviation becomes
exponentially harder to detect at a fixed n as dim grows. This is the
concrete, quantified version of the power/finite-sample-validity trade-off
that was only asserted in theory before. It is ALSO blind to
diagonal_correlated for a structural reason distinct from dimension: a
LINEAR functional's mean is invariant to any alternative that stays
symmetric about the box center along the tested direction (which
diagonal_correlated is, by construction) -- only the variance sub-test
could catch it, and evidently at n=400 the variance signal along a random
(not diagonal-aligned) direction is too weak relative to Hoeffding's
conservative bound to be picked up. Moment (mean/variance aggregated
per-axis) is ALSO blind to diagonal_correlated, for the different,
already-documented reason in section 3 (it never looks at cross-axis
structure at all). So at n=400, ONLY Weyl catches diagonal correlation,
for the specific reason that its frequency set was deliberately built to
include cross-axis (sum/difference) frequencies -- a random direction or a
per-axis moment aggregate both miss it, each for its own distinct reason.

CONCLUSION FOR THE PAPER: three criteria, three different power profiles,
none dominates:
  - moment: cheapest (already O(1) streaming, no design choices), good on
    symmetric shape changes, blind to cross-axis correlation.
  - Weyl: needs a deliberately chosen frequency set (axis + cross-axis),
    asymptotically exact calibration, best overall power here, has
    "blind harmonic" gaps at specific parameter values (a real, citable
    property of Fourier/Neyman-smooth GOF tests, not a flaw of this
    implementation).
  - random projection: the only one with an exact finite-sample (not just
    asymptotic) false-positive guarantee, but empirically the weakest
    criterion at the sample sizes used here, and its power degrades
    further, in a precisely quantifiable way, as dimension grows -- this
    is itself worth stating as a dimensionality result, symmetric to
    section 2's split-rule wall: SOME criteria (a literal 2^d-orthant
    test, and now also naive random-projection-with-a-generic-bound) get
    *worse* with dimension for reasons specific to the tool, not just "high
    dimension is hard" in general.

15. DENSITY-NORMALIZED DENSE/SPARSE THRESHOLD -- TRIED, MADE THINGS
    WORSE (2026-09-11)
================================================================
Implemented the #1 item from "Known limitations"/"Contributing": compare a
DENSITY (count per unit volume, rescaled to the root's volume so existing
threshold values stay in the same units) against dense_threshold/
sparse_threshold, instead of the raw decayed count s0. New
AdaptiveDStream(density_normalize=True) (default False, so nothing existing
changes); GridCell.state() takes a volume_scale multiplier
(domain_volume / cell.volume, = 1.0 unless enabled) applied to s0 before
the threshold comparison. Wired through dense_leaves(), _prune_recursive's
sparse check, _assign_clusters' transitional check, and plotting.py's two
call sites. See cell.py/model.py for the implementation, tests/test_model.py
for two new unit tests (::test_density_normalize_off_by_default_is_a_no_op,
::test_density_normalize_scales_effective_count_by_inverse_relative_volume).

RESULT: made ARI substantially WORSE on the headline stream, at every
threshold tried, not better. Probe (scratch, not committed --
make_varying_density_stream, n=2000, seed=7, same AdaptiveDStream config as
run_frontier_sweep.py: split_threshold=0.05, max_depth=7, max_cells=3000,
merge_threshold=0.01/merge_min_age=200):

  baseline (density_normalize=False, dense=0.5/sparse=0.05, current default):
    ARI=0.066  NMI=0.078  mem=673KB  unassigned=0.21
  density_normalize=True, dense/sparse swept together (same ratio as
  baseline, scaled by 1x/2x/4x/8x/16x/32x):
    dt=0.5  st=0.05   ARI=0.000  unassigned=0.02
    dt=1.0  st=0.1    ARI=0.001  unassigned=0.02
    dt=2.0  st=0.2    ARI=0.001  unassigned=0.02
    dt=4.0  st=0.4    ARI=0.002  unassigned=0.03
    dt=8.0  st=0.8    ARI=0.003  unassigned=0.04
    dt=16.0 st=1.6    ARI=0.006  unassigned=0.05

Every density_normalize configuration landed at ARI ~0 (chance), across two
orders of magnitude of threshold -- not a tuning problem, a mechanism
problem. Diagnosis: volume_scale = domain_volume/cell.volume =
2**(dim * level) for an axis-aligned cell split evenly to `level` (2D here,
so 2**(2*level)). At max_depth=7 that is up to 2**14 = 16384x. A threshold
multiplier that grows this fast with depth means almost ANY cell that has
split a few levels deep reads as "dense" the instant it holds even a
trace amount of decayed mass, regardless of whether that mass reflects
real local density or noise -- consistent with what's actually observed:
fraction_unassigned collapsed from 0.21 (baseline) to 0.02-0.05 (far MORE
points landing in a "cluster", not fewer), which is exactly the
over-aggressive-dense-classification failure mode, not the intended
"correctly recognize refined regions as still dense" fix. The normalization
is directionally motivated (fixes the real problem that raw count means
less every split) but the specific exponential-in-level scaling is too
aggressive uncalibrated/undamped -- it overshoots by orders of magnitude
well before max_depth is reached.

NOT ADOPTED. density_normalize stays implemented and available
(default off) for future iteration, but this session's straightforward
version is a negative result, not a fix -- do not cite this as an
improvement in the paper. Candidates for a next attempt, not yet tried:
  - a damped scale, e.g. sqrt(volume_scale) or log-scaled, rather than the
    full linear volume ratio;
  - cap volume_scale at some max multiplier (e.g. only normalize for the
    first few split levels, not all the way to max_depth);
  - normalize sparse_threshold and dense_threshold independently rather
    than by the same ratio (their optimal calibration may differ once
    density-normalized, since sparse's job -- gating pruning -- may not
    need the same aggressiveness as dense's job -- gating clustering);
  - re-tune split_threshold/merge_threshold alongside density_normalize
    rather than holding them at their raw-count-tuned values, since the
    interaction between "when to split" and "what counts as dense
    post-split" is exactly the coupling this fix targets.

17. RECENCY-WEIGHTED EVALUATION METRICS ADDED (2026-09-15, later same day)
================================================================
User objection (correct, with one factual correction applied): the flat
ARI/NMI/purity in EvalResult weight every point in the stream equally
regardless of arrival time, but AdaptiveDStream (and, it turns out,
DenStream and DBSTREAM too -- checked baselines.py: DenStream has
decaying_factor=0.02, DBSTREAM has fading_factor=0.01, both real exponential
fading, contrary to the user's stated assumption that only this method
decays) all maintain state that forgets old data on purpose. Only CluStream
(windowed snapshots via time_window/time_gap) genuinely doesn't decay. So
the precise issue isn't "AdaptiveDStream decays and nothing else does" --
it's that the FLAT METRIC reflects none of any model's recency behavior,
and three different models decay at three uncontrolled, non-comparable
rates (0.02, 0.01, and AdaptiveDStream's 0.99, not even on the same scale
without converting to effective sample size). A flat aggregate ARI/NMI
silently assumes "every timestep matters equally forever" and isn't a
neutral default just because it's the common one.

IMPLEMENTED in src/adaptive_dstream/evaluation.py (additive, opt-in, fully
backward compatible -- existing callers/scripts/outputs are unaffected):

  - decayed_purity_score(y_true, y_pred, decay): a recency-weighted purity.
    Point i (0-indexed, arrival order) gets weight decay**(n-1-i), so the
    most recent point has weight 1 and older points fade at exactly the
    rate GridCell.decay_to uses. Generalizes purity_score's per-cluster
    "does the majority true label match" logic to weighted sums instead of
    raw counts -- purity is a per-point statistic, so this is a direct,
    unambiguous weighted plug-in with no subtlety.
  - run_stream_eval(..., eval_decay: float | None = None): when provided,
    additionally computes decayed_purity (as above) plus a WINDOWED
    (not decay-weighted) ari_recent/nmi_recent -- plain, unmodified
    sklearn adjusted_rand_score/normalized_mutual_info_score recomputed
    over only the most recent recent_window = ceil(1/(1-eval_decay))
    points, where that formula is the effective sample size of an
    exponential fade at that rate (already established in sec. 8's decay
    table). eval_decay is a single value fixed by the CALLER and applied
    identically to every model in a sweep -- it does NOT read any
    individual model's own internal decay/fading parameter. This is a
    deliberate choice: the point is one controlled, external recency lens
    applied uniformly, not a different (and then incomparable) window per
    model.

WHY ARI ITSELF ISN'T DECAY-WEIGHTED DIRECTLY (documented in
decayed_purity_score's docstring, repeated here since it's the key design
decision): ARI is defined via combinatorial counts over PAIRS of points,
and its "adjusted for chance" correction assumes a specific null
distribution over contingency tables (generalized hypergeometric) that
changes once points carry unequal weights -- a naive weighted plug-in into
the standard ARI formula would NOT have a correct chance-correction term
under those weights, and deriving the correct one is a real, nontrivial
extension (related literature: weighted/fuzzy Rand indices, e.g. Campello
2007, but not a drop-in). Rather than risk shipping a subtly-wrong
"decayed ARI," the windowed (not weighted) ari_recent/nmi_recent uses
completely standard, trusted ARI/NMI math, just restricted to a
recency-focused slice of the stream -- correct by construction, at the
cost of a hard cutoff instead of a smooth fade. If a properly-derived
decay-weighted ARI is wanted later, this is the place to pick that back up,
not before working out the corrected chance-adjustment term.

RESULTS (all four re-run, 2026-09-15): the recency-weighted lens tells a
materially different story than the flat one, but NOT uniformly in
AdaptiveDStream's favor -- both directions are real findings, not just
the favorable one.

  Frontier sweep (varying_density, n=2000, seed=7) -- the gap NARROWS
  but does NOT close: AdaptiveDStream flat ARI=0.066 -> ARI_recent=0.582
  (a large jump), but at its own memory footprint (666KB, between
  FixedGrid n=22 and n=32), those two neighbors score ARI_recent=0.842
  and 1.000 respectively -- still clearly ahead. DBSTREAM (0.236->0.481)
  and DenStream (0.092->0.397) also both jump substantially. So on this
  specific stream, recency-weighting narrows every model's apparent
  deficit somewhat (the early portion of a drifting 3-phase stream is
  harder for everyone, before enough decayed mass has accumulated
  anywhere), but the fixed-grid frontier's dominance over AdaptiveDStream,
  documented throughout sec. 8/16 of this file, survives this lens too.

  Regime sweep (5 regimes, n=500, seed=7) -- mixed, and in AdaptiveDStream's
  favor more often than not: ARI -> ARI_recent moves from 0.123->0.214
  (static_no_drift), 0.109->0.029 (density_drift, WORSE), 0.041->0.247
  (two_gaussian_drift, a >6x jump), 0.004->0.040 (moons_drift), and
  0.030->0.026 (spatial_drift, flat). Four of five regimes improve or
  hold; density_drift is the one exception, and worth a specific note:
  it's the regime where the dense cluster's spread is WIDENING over time
  (dense_std_schedule=[0.15,0.15,1.1]), so the true "recent" ground truth
  there is itself the hardest-to-resolve phase (a newly-diffuse cluster) --
  ARI_recent getting worse there is arguably measuring something real about
  that regime's difficulty, not a flaw in the metric.

  Split-strategy sweep (n=800) and dimension sweep (n=400, dims 2-20):
  same qualitative pattern as the frontier sweep -- ARI_recent is higher
  than flat ARI for essentially every model (moment_based/equal_uniform/
  point_mass alike, and both FixedGrid and AdaptiveDStream across
  dimensions), consistent with "early-stream underperformance while decayed
  mass accumulates" being a real, shared effect across models rather than
  something specific to AdaptiveDStream. One dimension-sweep exception
  worth flagging: dim=10 is the one dimension where AdaptiveDStream's flat
  ARI (0.047) already slightly exceeds the fixed grid's (0.030), and
  ARI_recent preserves that ordering (0.129 vs 0.082) rather than reversing
  it -- for that one dimension the earlier "likely small-sample noise"
  read (see sec. 16... actually this dimension-sweep number, not sec. 16's
  regime-sweep one) gets a little more support from a second, independent
  metric agreeing on the direction, though still just one seed.
